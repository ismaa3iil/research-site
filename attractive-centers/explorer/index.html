A guide to attractive centers

Start with an ordinary average, replace numbers by points, and ask a simple question: when one input moves, can the average move against it? This explorer studies centers whose answer is “no”. Their possible positions lie between an inner heart and an outer chest.

From means of numbers to attractive centers of points

For real numbers, a monotone mean cannot decrease when one input increases. In the plane there is no single order “less than”: movement has a direction. Attractivity replaces scalar monotonicity by a directional test. If only one input moves by a vector d, and the center changes from X to X′, require

d · (X′ − X) ≥ 0.

The dot product measures the center's response along the movement. A perpendicular response is allowed, and equality is allowed. Attractivity does not mean that the center must follow a parallel path or move by a prescribed fraction of the input displacement. The centroid is the simplest example: moving one of three vertices by d moves it by d/3.

We also require geometric invariance: relabelling the inputs does not change the center; translating, rotating or reflecting the configuration transforms the center in the same way; scaling the configuration scales the center. A triangle center is described by normalized barycentric functions that depend on its side lengths. In the analytic research class, those functions are real analytic on the domain of nondegenerate triangles, with extension assumptions stated when a construction uses flat limits.

Write a = |BC|, b = |CA| and c = |AB|. A raw barycentric formula f gives weights f(a,b,c), f(b,c,a), f(c,a,b). Divide by their sum before forming the weighted position. Normalization matters: adding a constant to a raw formula generally differs from an affine combination of the actual center positions.

For a differentiable center, the local test is that the symmetric part of each vertex Jacobian is positive semidefinite. This means that every infinitesimal displacement gives a nonnegative directional response. Applying the test throughout configuration space, with the required regularity and extension, gives the finite-displacement property by integration. Passing tests at a finite set of triangles is evidence, but cannot establish that universal property.

Chest, Heart, BigHeart, and the optimal simple chest

A chest is a convex region containing every admissible attractive center at the given configuration. Intersecting proved chests gives a chest. The convex hull of the input points is the first example: reflection symmetry and parallel restoration from a supporting line or plane imply the corresponding half-space bound.

A heart is a convex region every point of which can be attained by an admissible attractive center map. Convex combinations of attractive maps are attractive, so the convex hull of a certified finite family gives an inner approximation. The choice of combination can depend on the point one wants to realize; once chosen, its coefficients are constant in the defining map.

The maximal Heart is the set of all attainable values. It is convex, but closedness and attainment of limiting boundary values require care. A BigHeart, in the project's terminology, is both a heart and a chest. No displayed finite family has been proved to give the maximal Heart for all triangles.

The optimal simple chest (OSC) is a research objective for a specified class of finite geometric inequalities. “Optimal” requires proving that no allowed construction can improve it. The standard triangle chest has a distinguished role in this investigation, but its identification with an OSC is conjectural. Likewise, the four-point and tetrahedron intersections in this app are finite constructions, without a completeness claim.

B36: what is proved, and what remains numerical?

B36 contains 34 globally defined rational triangle-center maps from the audited B41 catalogue and the two endpoint power maps M4−2√2 and M4+2√2. For rational source maps the notation is

F(λ, X(i)) = G + λ (X(i) − G),   G = (A + B + C)/3.

Thus λ = 0 gives G, λ = 1 gives X(i), negative λ lies opposite X(i) across G, and λ > 1 continues beyond it. A transformed map may be attractive even when X(i) itself is not. The two new generators are not ETC rational maps: they minimize the sum of the p-th powers of distances at the proved universal endpoints.

Current conclusion

The displayed B36 is a valid heart: each of its 36 generators is attractive, hence every fixed convex combination is attractive.

The reduction statement is not yet a theorem: extensive affine-conditioned scans found the seven extra B41 maps inside the hull of the 34 retained rational maps plus the two power endpoints. Across 1,288 audit shapes and 40 removal orders the same B36 survived, and a larger stress scan found no counterexample. A universal symbolic containment certificate is still required to prove that B36 always reproduces the B43 hull.

The seven removed B41 maps are F(−1/2,X(1476)), F(−1/2,X(4321)), F(2/3,X(4857)), F(3/7,X(7748)), F(−3/2,X(10000)), F(−1/2,X(9445)), and F(−1/2,X(9446)). Their removal does not threaten the validity of B36 as a heart; it affects only the unproved claim that no displayed B43 boundary is lost.

The rational audit itself remains useful: 449 rational maps in the investigated shortlist were proved attractive and almost all received containment certificates in the former base. The current construction does not exhaust ETC centers, centroidal parameters, power centers, or all attractive maps.

There are two separate proofs in this work. Attractivity certificates check Jacobian positivity on the entire triangle domain. Containment certificates express maps inside convex hulls with weights proved nonnegative for every triangle. The browser renders numerical positions from the certified formulas; it does not perform those proofs anew while you drag.

The always-attractive power family in every tab

The earlier approximation remains available, unchanged, as the rose dashed layer. It is the convex hull of the incenter X(1), the boundary-wire center X(10), the centroid X(2), and 49 sampled power centers Mp. A power center minimizes the sum of the p-th powers of its distances to the vertices. The app samples the interval 4 − 2√2 ≤ p ≤ 4 + 2√2, for which the project's theorem guarantees attractivity.

The same theorem applies to any finite number of points in any Euclidean dimension. The four-point tab therefore uses 49 exponent samples plus p=2 when needed; the tetrahedron tab uses 41 plus p=2. The numerical solver minimizes the strictly convex objective in normalized coordinates. A failed convergence is omitted and reported rather than silently plotted.

For noninteger p, the power maps are understood in the continuous/regular radial-center class used by the theorem. At collision strata they need not satisfy the stronger real-analytic extension convention imposed on some parts of the triangle-center project. The explorer therefore states which class supports each layer instead of silently identifying the two.

Why is the sampled convex hull a heart? Choose any displayed convex coefficients once and for all. Apply those same coefficients to the corresponding center maps at every configuration. Jacobians and finite attractivity inequalities combine linearly, so the resulting map remains attractive. The displayed hull is therefore rigorous modulo numerical evaluation of its generators. What sampling does not prove is that it captures the convex hull of the whole continuous p-family.

B36 uses only the two endpoint power maps, together with 34 rational maps. The isosceles power path is provably contained in the segment between those endpoints. Containment of the full power path in B36 for every scalene triangle is strongly supported numerically but remains an explicit conjecture.

Building the standard chest of a triangle

The standard chest is the intersection of three constructions: the original triangle, the characteristic side-normal strips, and the characteristic internal Napoleon triangle. The two extra layers can be shown with their checkboxes.

Side-normal strips. For each side, draw its perpendicular bisector and the parallel line through the opposite vertex. An isosceles predecessor forces the center onto its reflection axis. Moving the tip, or a suitable base vertex, parallel to the side produces the appropriate bound. The attractive center lies between the two lines. Intersect the three strips and the original triangle. The app displays the strips clipped to the triangle.

Internal Napoleon construction. Build an equilateral triangle on each side with its apex on the same side of the line as the opposite vertex. Take the three equilateral centroids, then the triangle whose side midpoints are those centroids: its vertices are N₂ + N₃ − N₁ and the two cyclic expressions. The symmetry-forced equilateral centers and their vertex displacements give the corresponding supporting half-planes. In an equilateral configuration this construction collapses to G. “Internal” describes the side choice; an equilateral apex need not lie inside the original triangle.

Why symmetry is useful. An invariant center must lie in every fixed subspace imposed by the configuration's symmetries. An equilateral triangle forces a single point; an isosceles triangle forces a line. Attractivity then transports a known projection bound from that symmetric predecessor to the target configuration.

Four points: what each checkbox adds

The four-point tab now displays two kinds of objects. The sampled power-center heart is an inner attainable region. Every other geometric method supplies a necessary outer bound; checking several chest methods intersects those bounds. The table reports each role explicitly.

  • Parallelogram strips: align the midpoints of two pairs, obtaining a centrally symmetric predecessor. Restore the pair in parallel. The center stays between the two midpoint-normal lines.
  • Useful bisectors: when the other two points lie on the same side of a pair's perpendicular bisector, reflection and parallel restoration give a half-plane.
  • Two-order network: begin with an equilateral triangle and its center, move the remaining inputs in both orders, and keep a separate unknown center at each intermediate configuration. Intermediate chest bounds and attractivity constrain the final center. The intermediate variables are essential; arbitrary nonparallel movements do not give independent final half-planes.
  • Involution orbit bounds: a finite selection of reflection and point-inversion predecessors supplies further symmetry constraints.
  • Edge-projection slabs: project onto each pair's edge direction. The center's projection lies between the minimum and maximum of the pair-midpoint projection and those of the remaining points.
  • Face-centroid transfer: inserting the centroid of a three-point configuration induces an attractive map on that configuration. If the fourth point is exactly the centroid, its triangle chest transfers in full. Otherwise restoring the fourth point gives a directional support bound.
  • Diameter balls: an optional bound for coherent convex power means, obtained from the insertion inequality (Y−X)·(Y−D) ≤ 0. It is a conditional family bound, represented by sampled support directions.
  • Absorption: an additional cross-arity assumption X₄(A,B,C,X₃(A,B,C)) = X₃(A,B,C). Its checkbox changes the assumptions; it is not part of attractivity alone.

Parallel movements of several inputs can be handled in sequence with a shared projection direction. For nonparallel movements the network must retain intermediate center values. The app marks intersections using diameter balls or absorption as conditional. The standard chest of A,B,C is an optional reference overlay, not automatically a four-point bound.

Tetrahedra, symmetry, and numerical limits

In three dimensions the same projection ideas produce planes and slabs. The tetrahedron tab implements opposite-edge slabs, useful bisectors, finite involution bounds, edge-projection slabs and face-centroid transfer. The optional diameter-ball bounds have the same power-family restriction. Recursive spatial network optimization is not implemented.

The green polyhedron is different: it is an inner convex hull of sampled tetrahedral power centers. A one-parameter curve can have a three-dimensional convex hull. For symmetric configurations the hull may collapse to a point, segment or flat polygon; zero volume therefore does not mean the heart is empty.

A tempting rule is to join a face's triangle chest to the opposite vertex and call the pyramid a chest. This is false in general; the counterexample preset illustrates why the app uses the justified face-centroid support construction instead.

Drag empty space to rotate the tetrahedron, and drag a vertex parallel to the screen. The coordinate fields allow exact placement along all three axes. Retained percentages refer to volume in this tab, and to area in the planar tabs. Zero volume can correspond to a nonempty lower-dimensional region. Collinear triangles and flat tetrahedra are reported explicitly; the display uses geometric tolerances for intersections and hulls.

Reproducibility, software, and further reading

This is free, MIT-licensed software. Its calculations run on your device, without a server, account, tracking or external JavaScript dependency. The rational evaluator uses published formulas after exact symbolic normalization, with integer polynomial evaluation to prevent cancellation. Power centers use a dimension-independent Newton method with backtracking. Three-dimensional hull faces are reconstructed from sampled supporting planes. Final drawings remain numerical.
